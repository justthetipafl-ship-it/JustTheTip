Drive

    How it works

    You take over on your own 35 with two minutes left, one score down and three timeouts. A touchdown wins it. A turnover, a fourth-down stop or the clock ending it ends your drive.

    Three things on this page are real and one is not, and it is worth being exact about which.

    Real

    The players. This week's actual depth charts for the clubs in that game. Who gets the ball. Each man's real share of his club's targets and carries this season, so the ball goes where it has actually been going. What happens on a snap. Every outcome is drawn from a real NFL fourth-quarter play — four seasons of them — filtered to the club you are playing and the call you made. A pass can be sacked at your club's own rate: the Chargers broke down on 11.2% of their fourth-quarter dropbacks over those seasons, the Bills on 5.0%.

    Not real

    The first three quarters. That box score is invented so a drive can matter: a two-minute drill on its own produces a few dozen yards, which could never reach a receiving or passing line. It is built from each man's real per-game form, which makes it plausible, and it is not a projection, a forecast or a result. It is the same every time you play a given game and side, so you cannot reroll it until it suits you.

    The clock is also a model. It does not tick while you think — it moves only when you call a play. A completed pass or a run burns 24 seconds at hurry-up tempo; an incompletion or a sack costs six, because the clock stops. Real plays were chained in real drives and this draws them from different games, so their real elapsed times cannot be reused.

    Your three timeouts are spent after a play, not before, which is how it works on the field: when a run or a completion leaves the clock going, stopping it hands back the 18 seconds you would have burned before the next snap. The button only lights up when there is a clock to stop, so a timeout can never be thrown away on an incompletion that stopped it anyway.

    For the curious: a sensible play-caller wins about one drive in six, a player picking at random about one in nine. That gap is the whole game, and it is why the clock sits at 24 seconds rather than lower — tested further down, blind deep bombs start out-scoring good calls.
