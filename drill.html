    How it works

    You start on your own 35, one score down, two minutes on the clock, three timeouts. A touchdown wins it. A turnover, a fourth-down stop or the clock hitting zero ends it.

    When you make a call, the drill finds a real play your team ran on a similar down and distance and gives you what actually happened — the yards, the receiver, the sack. Nothing is simulated and nothing is invented. Each result shows the game it came from, so you can go and look it up.

    A pass can be sacked because sacks are in the pool at your club's own rate: the Chargers broke down on 11.2% of their fourth-quarter dropbacks over these four seasons, the Bills on 5.0%. That difference is real, and you feel it.

    What the drill approximates

    One thing is not real, and it would be dishonest to leave it unsaid. The clock is a model, not replayed data: a completed pass or a run burns 24 seconds at hurry-up tempo, an incompletion or a sack stops it at six, and a timeout stops it dead. Real plays were chained in real drives, and this drill draws them from different games, so their real elapsed times cannot be reused. Everything else — every yard, name, sack and turnover — is a real play.

    For the curious: a sensible play-caller wins about one drive in six, a player picking at random about one in nine. That gap is the whole game, and it is why the clock sits at 24 seconds rather than lower — tested far enough down and blind deep bombs start out-scoring good calls.
